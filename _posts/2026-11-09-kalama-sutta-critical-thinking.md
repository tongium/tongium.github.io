Most Thai kids hear about **กาลามสูตร** (the Kalama Sutta) at some point in school. Usually in one line: *"Don't believe anything just because someone told you."*

That line is true, but it sells the text short. When I went back and read it as an adult, it looked less like a moral lesson and more like a checklist. A very old, very specific checklist for spotting the moment you're about to believe something for the wrong reason.

And honestly, it fits 2026 better than it fit my school days.

---

### The Setup

The story is short. The Kalamas were people living in a town called Kesaputta, on a busy road. Teachers came through all the time. Each one praised his own teaching and tore down everyone else's.

So the Kalamas went to the Buddha with a very reasonable question: *"They all sound convincing. They all say the others are wrong. How are we supposed to know who's telling the truth?"*

Replace "wandering teachers" with "your feed," and that's the same question most of us are asking every day.

---

### The Ten "Don'ts"

The answer is a list of ten things that should **not, on their own,** be your reason for believing something. Here they are, with what they look like today.

| # | Pali | Don't believe it just because... | What it looks like now |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | มา อนุสฺสเวน | you've heard it over and over | "Everyone keeps saying it" |
| 2 | มา ปรมฺปราย | it's tradition | "We've always done it this way" / "It's best practice" |
| 3 | มา อิติกิราย | it's rumor or news going around | The screenshot in the group chat |
| 4 | มา ปิฏกสมฺปทาเนน | it's in a respected text | "It's in the docs" / "There's a paper" |
| 5 | มา ตกฺกเหตุ | it's logical | A clean argument from shaky premises |
| 6 | มา นยเหตุ | it can be inferred | "The trend line says..." |
| 7 | มา อาการปริวิตกฺเกน | it seems reasonable at first glance | It *sounds* right |
| 8 | มา ทิฏฺฐินิชฺฌานกฺขนฺติยา | it agrees with what you already think | Confirmation bias |
| 9 | มา ภพฺพรูปตาย | the speaker seems credible | Confident tone, nice slides, a big title |
| 10 | มา สมโณ โน ครูติ | "this is our teacher" | Your favorite founder, influencer, or tech lead |

A few things jump out.

**Number 4 includes scripture.** A religious text telling you not to believe something just because it's in a religious text. That's unusual, and it's on purpose.

**Number 10 includes the Buddha himself.** He was the teacher in the room. The text doesn't make an exception for him.

**Numbers 5 and 6 include logic.** This is the part I missed as a kid, and it's the part that makes it more than "think for yourself."

---

### Wait, Not Even Logic?

Modern critical thinking leans hard on logic. So why would a text about clear thinking tell you not to trust it?

Because logic only checks that the steps connect. It doesn't check that the starting point is true. A perfectly valid argument built on a wrong assumption gives you a wrong answer with total confidence. Anyone who has debugged a system knows this feeling: every line of code is correct, and the whole thing is still broken, because the assumption on line one was wrong.

Logic is a tool. It's not a verdict. Being able to argue for something isn't the same as it being true. (Smart people are often *better* at this, which is exactly why they can be so confidently wrong.)

---

### So What *Should* You Do?

This is the second half that usually gets cut off.

The Kalama Sutta isn't "believe nothing." It's not "only trust your own experience" either. That would fail check number 8: what you already think is on the list too.

What it actually says is roughly this: **when you know for yourselves that something is**

*   **unwholesome** (อกุศล),
*   **blameworthy** (มีโทษ),
*   **criticized by the wise** (วิญญูชนติเตียน),
*   **and leads to harm and suffering when you put it into practice,**

**then let it go.** And when you know something is the opposite, take it up and live by it.

I read that as three tests, and they map surprisingly well onto how good engineering teams make decisions:

1.  **Look at the results, not the pitch.** What actually happens when people put this into practice? Not what's promised. What happens. This is the closest thing in the text to "run the experiment."
2.  **Check it with people who know.** Not "people who agree with me." People whose judgment you'd trust *even when they disagree with you*. That's peer review. It's also code review, when it's done properly.
3.  **Own the conclusion.** "Know for yourselves" doesn't mean "figure it out alone." It means you can't outsource the final decision. You looked, you checked, you're responsible.

None of the ten "don'ts" are banned as *inputs*. Tradition, texts, logic and good teachers are all useful. They're just not allowed to be the *end* of the thinking.

### The New Teacher in the Room

Here's why this feels so current to me.

When I ask an AI model a question, the answer usually hits several items on that list at once:

*   It sounds reasonable (#7).
*   It's well structured and logical (#5).
*   It's written in a confident, credible voice (#9).
*   It often cites something, or sounds like it could (#4).
*   And if I phrased my question with a bias, it'll happily agree with me (#8).

That's five out of ten, in one reply, in a few seconds. No wandering teacher in Kesaputta was that efficient.

I'm not saying don't use it. I use it every day, and I wrote about what happens when people stop checking its output in [AI Slop]({% post_url 2026-10-12-ai-slop-and-dark-jokes %}) and [PR Storm]({% post_url 2026-11-03-pr-storm %}). The point is that **"the AI said so"** is just a new version of **"our teacher said so."** It's item 10 with a chat box.

The Kalama test still works on it. Did I check the result? Did someone who knows the area look at it? Am I willing to put my name on it?

---

### The Part About Not Knowing

There's one more section near the end that I like a lot, and it rarely gets mentioned.

After all this, the text describes a person who lives by what's wholesome, and says they gain four kinds of comfort (อัสสาสะ 4). Roughly:

*   *If* there is a next life and karma is real, I'll be fine.
*   *If* there isn't, I'm still living well and happily here and now.
*   *If* bad things come to those who do bad, I haven't done any.
*   *If* they don't, I'm clean either way.

In other words: **you don't need to win the metaphysical argument to make a good decision.** Pick the path that works out well across all the possibilities you can't settle yet.

That's not a dodge. It's one of the most practical ideas in the whole thing. In software we'd call it a decision that's robust to uncertainty. You don't know which way the market, the framework or the token price will go, so you choose the option you won't regret in any of them.

---

### What I'd Actually Do

*   **When you feel sure, ask "why do I believe this?"** If the answer is one of the ten items, you're not done thinking yet.
*   **Pay extra attention to #8.** The beliefs that feel most obviously true are usually the ones you never checked.
*   **Separate "sounds right" from "works."** Then go look at whether it works.
*   **Find people who'll tell you you're wrong.** Keep them close. They're rarer than people who'll tell you you're right.
*   **Treat AI answers like a smart, fast stranger.** Useful, often right, never the final word.
*   **When you can't know, choose what's good either way.**

---

### The Short Version

*   The Kalama Sutta lists ten reasons that are **not enough on their own** to believe something: repetition, tradition, rumor, texts, logic, inference, appearances, your own views, credible speakers, and your own teacher.
*   It doesn't say "believe nothing." It says test it: look at the real results, check with wise people, and own the conclusion.
*   Even logic and the Buddha himself don't get a free pass.
*   AI answers hit half the list in one go, which makes this old checklist more useful now, not less.
*   When the big questions can't be settled, choose the path that's good in every case.

The Kalamas had a road full of confident teachers. We have a feed full of them, and now a chat box too. The question hasn't changed: not *who* do I believe, but *how* do I check.

Which leaves me with one I can't settle. An AI model can recite the ten "don'ts," explain them, and even warn you not to trust its own answers. But it doesn't see what happens when you act on its advice, and it never has to own the result.

So I'll leave it with you: **do you think AI has critical thinking?**
