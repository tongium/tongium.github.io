Growing up, there were certain fruits that were just a normal part of childhood. I remember the sharp, tongue-tingling sourness of bilimbi plucked straight from a backyard tree, or the crisp, juicy ridges of star fruit that bruised if you so much as looked at them the wrong way. They weren't perfectly shaped. They were fragile, messy, and sometimes overwhelmingly tart. But the taste was vibrant and felt entirely alive.

Today, I can hardly find them anywhere.

Much of the everyday fruit I remember eating when I was young seems to have vanished. It has been quietly replaced by imported apples that look like they were polished in a factory, strawberries the size of small plums, and grapes that are reliably seedless but taste mostly of water. 

It makes me wonder: why can't I buy the fruit I grew up with anymore? Where did it go?

My first instinct is to blame capitalism. The relentless logic of the modern supermarket requires uniformity, predictability, and above all, shelf life. A delicate star fruit that bruises easily or a wildly sour fruit that spoils in a few days is a liability. This economic pressure forces a harsh reality: farmers naturally pivot to planting the most commercially valuable, high-yield crops—like heavily demanded sweet fruits—rather than maintaining a diverse, flavorful array of traditional variants. The agricultural industry prioritizes durability, pest resistance, and profit over taste. The messy, intensely flavored fruits of my childhood simply didn't survive the brutal efficiency of the global supply chain. 

But maybe the internet is also to blame. We live in an era where the algorithm demands aesthetics. We eat with our eyes first, and a bruised, oddly-shaped heirloom fruit doesn't photograph well. The expectation of perfection has trickled down from our feeds into our grocery carts. We have been subtly trained to reject the blemished and the irregular. Have our own digital desires for the picture-perfect reshaped the genetics of our orchards?

Or perhaps the truth is something far more existential: global warming. The seasons are shifting. The delicate balance of frost and sun that gave those old fruits their distinct, concentrated character might no longer exist. The soil composition is changing, the water cycles are disrupted, and the pollinators are struggling. The climate that nurtured the trees I picked from as a kid is simply not the same climate we live in today. The fruit isn't the same because the earth isn't the same.

Maybe it's a combination of all three. A perfect storm of economic optimization, aesthetic obsession, and ecological shift. 

But sometimes, when I stand in the brightly lit produce aisle, staring at a pyramid of identical, flawless, tasteless fruit, I can't help but wonder. 

Is the fruit really gone? Or did we just forget how to tolerate imperfection in exchange for convenience? And if we traded away the taste of our childhood for a longer shelf life, what else are we slowly giving up without even realizing it?
